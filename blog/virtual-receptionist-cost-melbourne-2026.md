## What does a virtual receptionist actually do?

## What about offshore call centres?

Offshore call centres (often called "virtual receptionist services" in marketing) typically charge:

- **Shared plan:** $800-1,500/month for a block of minutes (say 200-300 minutes), shared across multiple clients.
- **Dedicated plan:** $2,000-4,000/month for a named receptionist or small team who knows your business.
- **Setup fees:** $200-1,000 one-off.
- **Per-call or per-minute overage:** $0.80-2.00 per call or $0.30-0.60/minute once you blow the bundle.

What you get for that money: real humans, usually based in the Philippines, South Africa, or India, who answer in an Australian or American accent. They can take messages, transfer urgent calls, and do basic data entry. They generally can't integrate with your Cal.com calendar to book live (some can with effort), and Australian English slang is a common friction point.

For a Melbourne tradie who just needs every call answered and a message taken, they're a perfectly reasonable step up from voicemail. The downsides are the monthly minimum (you pay even on quiet weeks), the per-call overage (a busy month gets expensive fast), and the fact that the receptionist doesn't actually know your business beyond a one-page brief.

## What drives the cost difference?

Three things, mainly:

1. **Labour cost.** A human's time is bounded by the 38-hour week and the award rate. An AI's time is bounded by the per-minute voice inference cost (about $0.10/min all-in at Amily's stack using ElevenLabs + Twilio [Source: Amily AI tech stack, 2026]).
2. **Capacity utilisation.** A human on $60k/year costs the same whether they take 5 calls or 50. An AI costs more when it's busier but the marginal cost per call is tiny.
3. **Setup depth.** A human needs a desk, a laptop, payroll, and a manager. An AI needs a Cal.com account and 30 minutes of configuration.

There's a fourth, less obvious driver: the [Privacy Act 2024 amendments](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-the-privacy-and-the-use-of-commercially-available-ai-products) require Australian businesses to disclose automated decision-making by **10 December 2026** [Source: OAIC, 2024]. That means any AI service you buy in 2026 needs to come with proper disclosure wording, a privacy policy template, and a clear escalation path to a human. Cheap offshore AI services often can't produce that documentation. Australian-built ones can.
