## Does my small business actually have to follow the Privacy Act 2024?

Yes, if you trade in Australia and handle personal information, you fall under the Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) as amended by the Privacy Act 2024. The small-business exemption (the old "annual turnover of less than $3 million" carve-out) was narrowed, and from late 2024 most small businesses are treated as "APP entities" once they collect, hold, or trade personal information — which an AI receptionist does the moment it picks up a call. [Source: Office of the Australian Information Commissioner, Privacy Act reforms 2024]

For a tradie or clinic, "personal information" includes the caller's name, phone number, the job address they dictate, and any payment or health details they mention. The moment your AI books that job or sends an SMS confirmation, you've handled personal information for an APP purpose.

## What's changing on 10 December 2026 that I should care about?

On **10 December 2026**, the automated decision-making (ADM) provisions of the Privacy Act 2024 come into full effect. From that date, if a business uses an automated system to make a decision that has a legal or similarly significant effect on a person, it must:

- Tell the person that an automated system is being used.
- Explain, in plain language, how the decision was made and what it was based on.
- Offer a way to request human review of the decision.

For an AI receptionist, "a decision that has a legal or similarly significant effect" can include deciding a caller is a non-urgent lead, dropping a call to voicemail, routing a job as low-priority, or rejecting a booking. The OAIC's draft guidance treats these triage-style calls as in-scope. [Source: OAIC, Privacy Act 2024 ADM guidance, 2024]

If your current provider can't build those disclosures into the call opening and produce an audit trail, you're exposed on day one of the deadline.

## Where do US-based virtual receptionists usually fall short under the APPs?

US services like Ruby, Smith.ai and AnswerConnect are excellent at answering calls, but their data handling was designed for US defaults. Three recurring gaps show up in their terms and security pages:

1. **Data residency** — calls are typically handled and stored in the United States. That triggers APP 8 (cross-border disclosure). You can still use them, but you must tell the caller, include it in your privacy policy, and ensure the receiving country has substantially similar protections or you have consent.
2. **Recording and consent defaults** — US one-party-consent states don't need a clear "this call is recorded" opener. Victoria's Surveillance Devices act 1999 and the APPs expect you to tell the caller up front. [Source: Victoria SDA 1999, Office of the Chief Examiner]
3. **AI disclosure** — many US services use AI in the background but present the receptionist as a "real person". After December 2026, that mismatch is an ADM-transparency problem in Australia.

None of this is exotic to fix in principle. In practice, the contracts, default workflows, and infrastructure choices of a US-based vendor are tuned for US law. The Australian small business is the one left translating.

## What does an APP-native AI receptionist look like?

An AI receptionist built for Australia from day one should tick these boxes:

- **Resident infrastructure** — voice processing, transcripts and recordings stored in Australia (typically Sydney or Melbourne AWS or Azure regions).
- **Explicit AI disclosure** — the very first thing the caller hears is "G'day, I'm Amily, an AI receptionist for [Business]. This call may be recorded for quality and training." No pretending to be human.
- **APP 8 ready** — the privacy policy and the call script both disclose cross-border handling (or, better, avoid it entirely).
- **ADM transparency** — a clear, recorded statement that an automated system is making the triage, plus an easy path to a human.
- **Caller access and correction** — transcripts and call logs exportable to the caller on request (APP 12, APP 13).
- **Retention controls** — recordings auto-purged on a schedule the business chooses, not "indefinitely by default".

## Is the US receptionist option ever the right call?

Sometimes, yes. If your business already has its data in a US SaaS, your callers are mostly US-based, and you have the in-house capacity to keep a privacy policy current, the gap is small. For a typical Melbourne plumber, dental clinic, café or physio, the gap is large enough that the legal review and policy upkeep will cost more than the difference in subscription price between a US service and an Australian-native one.
