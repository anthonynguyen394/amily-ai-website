## What is an AI receptionist for a law firm?

An AI receptionist for a law firm is a voice agent that answers your practice's line 24/7 in natural speech: it greets callers with your firm's name, discloses that it is AI, runs your approved intake questions, books consultations into your calendar, and escalates urgent matters to the on-call practitioner — without ever giving legal advice.

On a routine call it does what a good front-desk person does: captures the caller's name and contact details, identifies the matter type, notes other parties for conflicts screening, and books a consultation via a configured API workflow with Cal.com, Google Calendar, or a practice tool with a booking API. The part that matters for a legal practice is triage. A caller whose spouse has just been detained, or whose visa expires on Monday, is not offered a slot on Thursday — the system emails or texts the on-call practitioner within seconds, with a summary of what the caller said. Equally important is what it will not do: no advice, no opinions on merits, no outcome estimates. It collects facts and moves the caller to a human. ## How much do missed calls cost an Australian law firm?

More than most principals think, because the caller who cannot reach you simply retains the firm that answers. In Clio's 2024 secret-shopper study of 500 law firms, only 40% answered a prospective client's call — down from 56% in 2019 — and 48% were unreachable by phone [Source: Clio Legal Trends Report, 2024].

The same study found 64% of prospective clients received no follow-up at all, and 73% of the secret shoppers would not recommend the firms they contacted [Source: Clio Legal Trends Report, 2024]. Client expectations run the other way: 79% expect a response within 24 hours [Source: Clio Legal Trends Report, 2019]. Unlike a tradie's missed call — worth a few hundred dollars in [lost job value](/blog/cost-of-missed-calls-melbourne-tradies) — a missed new-matter call is a lost retainer worth thousands in fees, plus the referral tail that follows a well-run matter. The structure of the profession makes this worse. Of the 16,793 private law practices in Australia, 78% are sole practices and another 13% have two to four principals [Source: Law Society of NSW National Profile of Solicitors, 2024]. That is nine in ten firms with nobody spare to sit on the phones — and in family law, migration and criminal practice, the calls that matter most arrive precisely when nobody can answer: the Saturday-night arrest, the mid-hearing detention call, the 9pm visa expiry panic.

## Can an AI receptionist keep legal calls confidential?

Yes — but only if you demand four controls in writing: the AI discloses itself at the start of every call; call recordings and transcripts are never used to train models; data stays resident in Australia; and you hold purge-on-request rights. A provider that cannot evidence all four is not fit for legal intake.

The stakes are higher for a law practice than for any other small business. A solicitor's duty of confidentiality under rule 9 of the Australian Solicitors' Conduct Rules covers information acquired in practice, and communications made for the dominant purpose of seeking legal advice can attract legal professional privilege — including what a distressed caller blurts out before any retainer exists. So treat every intake call as potentially privileged, and hold the phone system to the same standard as your document management. The Victorian Legal Services Board and Commissioner's joint statement on AI in legal practice makes the regulator's position plain: lawyers cannot safely enter confidential, sensitive or privileged client information into public AI tools, and must run clear, risk-based policies over any AI they do use [Source: Victorian Legal Services Board and Commissioner, 2024].

A dedicated AI receptionist is a different architecture from pasting client details into a public chatbot — calls run through a contracted service with your firm's configuration — but the regulator's logic still applies: know where the data goes, who can access it, and how it is destroyed. The Law Institute of Victoria's AI guidance takes the same line [Source: Law Institute of Victoria, 2024]. Concretely: contract terms covering no-training use of call data, Australian hosting for recordings and transcripts, defined retention with purge controls, and breach notification. ## What do the Privacy Act and Victorian law require when an AI answers your phones?

Three specific obligations: notify callers at the point of collection (APP 5), disclose any recording at the start of the call (Surveillance Devices Act 1999 (Vic) s.6), and — from 10 December 2026 — disclose automated decision-making in your privacy policy under the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024 [Source: OAIC, 2024].

In practice, the first two collapse into one sentence of the greeting: "Hi, you've reached [Firm]. I'm an AI assistant and this call may be recorded." That covers APP 5 notification under the [Australian Privacy Principles](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles) and in-call disclosure under section 6 of the Surveillance Devices Act 1999 (Vic) — your firm is a party to calls to its own number, so recording is lawful with disclosure, and section 11 restricts sharing recordings downstream. APP 11 adds the security layer: recordings and transcripts encrypted in transit and at rest, access-controlled, and hosted onshore. The OAIC's 2024 AI guidance adds the diligence layer: know what a product does with your data before deploying it [Source: OAIC, 2024]. The third obligation has a hard deadline: from 10 December 2026, an APP entity using computer programs in decisions that significantly affect individuals must say so in its privacy policy [Source: OAIC, 2024]. An AI receptionist that triages urgency and books consultations sits squarely in scope; the fix is a paragraph of policy copy from your provider.

## What should an AI receptionist handle for a law firm — and what must it never do?

The dividing line is facts versus advice. The AI handles intake, booking, urgency triage, and practical questions about the firm. It must never answer "what should I do?", assess a matter's merits, or quote fees beyond your published fixed prices. That boundary is configured as a hard rule, not a tendency.

| The AI should handle | The AI must never do |
|---|---|
| Answer every call 24/7, disclose itself, greet with the firm's name | Give legal advice — ever |
| New-matter intake: caller details, matter type, other parties, urgency | Opine on merits, prospects, or strategy |
| Book consultations via a configured API workflow (Cal.com, Google Calendar, practice tools with a booking API) | Quote fees beyond published fixed prices |
| Escalate urgent matters to the on-call practitioner within seconds | Take instructions or make undertakings for the firm |
| Answer practical questions — hours, location, parking, what to bring | Discuss an existing client's file with a third party |
| Take structured messages and route them to the right practitioner | Guess — below its confidence threshold, it escalates |

Two design details carry most of the risk. The escalation matrix defines which phrases page a human immediately — "arrested", "detained", "intervention order", "visa expires" — while everything else books into the next available consultation. The refusal script handles callers who push for advice: the AI promises a practitioner callback, confirms the window, and logs the request — exactly what a good legal secretary already says. The broader division of labour is covered in [AI receptionist vs human receptionist](/blog/ai-receptionist-vs-human-receptionist): the AI takes the coverage problem, humans keep everything requiring judgement.

## How do you implement an AI receptionist in a small firm?

Plan a two-to-four week rollout: map your call types, write the escalation matrix, lock the disclosure script and data controls, connect the calendar, update your privacy policy, then pilot after-hours only before extending to daytime overflow. Here is the checklist we use with professional-services firms.

1. **Map your call types for a fortnight.** Tally new-matter enquiries, existing-client calls, courts and counsel, and genuinely urgent matters. This tells you what the intake script must cover.
2. **Write the escalation matrix.** List the matter types and trigger phrases that page the on-call practitioner immediately, and define the channel — email, SMS, or both.
3. **Approve the disclosure script.** One greeting sentence covers the AI disclosure and recording notice under APP 5 and the Surveillance Devices Act 1999 (Vic) s.6. A principal signs it off, not the vendor.
4. **Verify the four data controls in writing.** Australian data residency, no model training on call data, defined retention with purge-on-request, and breach notification — in the service agreement.
5. **Connect the calendar via a configured API workflow.** Cal.com or Google Calendar are the fast path; practice tools that expose a booking API are wired in during setup ([how the setup process works](https://amily.ai/#setup)).
6. **Update your privacy policy for automated decision-making** ahead of the 10 December 2026 deadline [Source: OAIC, 2024]. Your provider should hand you the paragraph.
7. **Pilot after-hours and overflow only for 30 days.** Divert the line outside business hours, review every transcript weekly, and tune the intake questions and escalation triggers.
8. **Extend to daytime overflow once the escalation tree is proven.** Most firms keep humans on first pickup during office hours and let the AI catch everything they cannot.
